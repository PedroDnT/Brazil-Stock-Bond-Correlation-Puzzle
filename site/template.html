A Floor, Not a Spike

Brazilian government bonds are diversifiers, not hedges. Across two decades and six macro regimes the correlation never turns reliably negative — that floor, not a crisis-time spike, is what distinguishes Brazil.

The switch is shock type, not crisis

Duration diversifies global risk-off episodes and amplifies domestic fiscal-political ones. NTN-B returned +5.8pp over CDI through the GFC and +1.3pp through COVID; the only two episodes where it lost to cash — Joesley Day and the 2024 fiscal scare — were both domestic. In the daily left tail, though, the diversification does fail: lower-tail dependence is 0.143 against the 0.050 implied by independence, so stocks and bonds crash together nearly three times as often as chance, and conditional on the worst decile of equity days ρ doubles to +0.279. No allocation within domestic fixed income turns these bonds into a hedge. Against a domestic fiscal shock the hedges are short LFT and international assets — and long LFT is not the risk-free instrument it is usually assumed to be, drawing down 1.33% at its longest maturity against 0.008% at one year.

Qualifications

  • No regime is statistically distinguishable from the calmest — all bootstrap p > 0.05. The post-2013 upward drift is suggestive, not established.
  • Most crisis correlation spikes do not survive the Forbes-Rigobon volatility adjustment: COVID falls from +0.370 raw to +0.124, and the GFC shows no elevation at all.
  • Gaussian VaR understates tail risk given the kurtosis in these series and should be read as a lower bound; the Joesley window is 11 trading days, so its estimate is imprecise.
  • Regime boundaries are researcher-defined rather than estimated, and the §9 global-macro extension was not reproduced — those claims are open, not established.
